# **App Description**

> **Status of this document.** This is the pedagogical foundation: the four competency libraries, the CEFR curriculum, and the exercise templates. It defines *what* is taught. `Session_Generation_Engine.md` defines *how* these three parts connect and how a session is assembled from a user profile; where the two disagree on mechanics, the engine document is authoritative. Several refinements recommended below (sub-competency IDs, prerequisite graphs, the playlist state model) are implemented there.

A language education application that closely resembles how natural language acquisition happens. And the learning design on the application is based off of three foundational fundamentals of learning science: Immediate Feedback (a user is corrected and made to retry after the correct answer is revealed or encouraged immediately if correct using AI), Metacognition (a user is prompted to reflect back on what error they were corrected on), and Spaced Retrieval (a user is presented with previous activities and topics with the period of space determined using algorithmic calculation based on the data collected from the user during the session of the activity).

## **Design recommendation for Version 2 — implemented**

This recommendation has been applied. Every vocabulary sub-competency now carries a unique ID, matching the grammar, pronunciation, and fluency libraries:

V009 Travel & Tourism  
│  
├── V009.01 Airport  
├── V009.02 Hotel  
├── V009.03 Booking  
├── V009.04 Immigration  
├── V009.05 Sightseeing  
├── V009.06 Travel Problems  
├── V009.07 Navigation  
└── V009.08 Cultural Tourism

This gives two levels of granularity:

* **High-level IDs (e.g., `V009`)** for curriculum mapping in Layer 1\.  
* **Sub-competency IDs (e.g., `V009.06`)** for generating and tracking individual practice sessions.

The session generator addresses everything at the sub-competency level, so this was a precondition for automated generation rather than a refinement. See `Session_Generation_Engine.md` section 2.

## **Recommended refinement before freezing**

Compared to the Vocabulary Library, I would make one important adjustment for Grammar.

Grammar has natural **prerequisites**, which your adaptive engine can exploit. Instead of treating the grammar library as a flat list, model it as a directed graph.

For example:

G005 Present Simple  
        │  
        ▼  
G006 Present Continuous  
        │  
        ▼  
G007 Past Simple  
        │  
        ▼  
G012 Present Perfect

And:

G021 Conjunctions & Complex Clauses  
        │  
        ▼  
G017 Relative Clauses  
        │  
        ▼  
G018 Conditionals  
        │  
        ▼  
G024 Inversion

This prerequisite graph allows your learning planner to enforce progression ("don't introduce Present Perfect before Past Simple") while still letting the spaced repetition system review earlier competencies independently. It also makes adaptive planning much easier when a learner struggles with an advanced topic—you can trace the difficulty back to prerequisite competencies rather than repeatedly drilling the same surface-level grammar.

# **Learning Engine Design**

1\. Stimulus  
        ↓  
2\. Comprehension  
        ↓  
3\. Production  
        ↓  
4\. AI Feedback  
        ↓  
5\. Retry  
        ↓  
6\. Reflection  
        ↓  
7\. Store Learning Record  
        ↓  
8\. Spaced Retrieval in a New Context

## **Stage 1 — Stimulus**

Purpose

Provide meaningful input.

Possible forms

* Image  
* Short audio  
* Conversation  
* Story  
* Video clip  
* Situation  
* Real-life object

Example

(Image)

A child dropping ice cream.

---

## **Stage 2 — Comprehension**

Before speaking, confirm the learner understands the stimulus.

Examples

"What happened?"

"What do you notice?"

"Who is crying?"

Children always understand before they produce.

---

## **Stage 3 — Production**

Now they speak.

Depending on level:

Beginner

> Name three things.

Intermediate

> Describe the picture.

Advanced

> Explain why the child reacted this way.

Notice that the stimulus stays the same while the task changes.

---

## **Stage 4 — Immediate Feedback**

This is where AI shines.

Instead of simply saying

"Wrong."

It can separate feedback into layers.

Vocabulary

"You used *sad*. A more natural word here is *upset*."

Grammar

"You should say *fell*, not *falled*."

Pronunciation

"Your stress should be on the first syllable."

Fluency

"You paused for several seconds."

---

## **Stage 5 — Retry**

One correction rarely changes long-term behavior.

The learner immediately retries.

This mirrors how children often repeat corrected language almost instantly.

---

## **Stage 6 — Reflection (Missing from your model)**

This is surprisingly powerful.

Ask one simple question.

"What changed?"

or

"What was your mistake?"

The learner verbalizes the correction.

That strengthens encoding.

---

## **Stage 7 — Store Weaknesses**

Don't store everything.

Store only things that matter.

Examples

User:

incorrect:  
"I goed."

correct:  
"I went."

Category:  
Past tense

Difficulty:  
Easy

Attempts:  
2

Last seen:  
Today

Or

Pronunciation

Word:  
comfortable

Problem:  
Stress

Your database slowly becomes a personalized model of the learner.

---

## **Stage 8 — Spaced Retrieval in a New Context**

This is where memory becomes durable.

Suppose yesterday the learner practiced

"I spilled my coffee."

Tomorrow

Show a different image.

Milk spilling.

Ask

"What happened?"

The learner now says

"He spilled his milk."

Same grammar.

Different context.

# **What should a Practice Session look like?**

I'd make every session have exactly the same schema.

Session

├── Session ID  
├── Skill  
├── Exercise Template  
├── CEFR Level  
├── Learning Objective  
├── Stimulus  
├── Prompt  
├── Expected Duration  
├── Evaluation Metrics  
├── Retry Rules  
├── Reflection Prompt  
└── Spaced Review Rule

Every activity follows this.

Automation becomes trivial.

Three of these fields were named here but never defined. They now are, in `Session_Generation_Engine.md`:

* **Evaluation Metrics** — the session's target sub-competency IDs joined to each competency's success criteria and common errors, weighted by the template's observation reliability.  
* **Retry Rules** — triggered when a targeted competency scores below 0.6; maximum two retries, each climbing one rung of the template's scaffold ladder; retry outcomes count at 0.6 weight.  
* **Spaced Review Rule** — retrievability decay, `R = exp(-elapsed / stability)`, due below 0.85, with stability growing from performance rather than a fixed interval ladder.

# **Now the important question:**

> How many activities should be generated?

I don't think in terms of days.

I think in terms of **learning load**.

For speaking, cognitive fatigue is real. After about 30–45 minutes of focused speaking practice, the quality of output tends to decline. That suggests each day should have a fixed "budget" rather than a fixed number of lessons.

For example:

| Level | Daily Time | Sessions | New Concepts | Reviews |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| A2 | 20–25 min | 4 | 2–3 | 2 |
| B1 | 25–35 min | 4–5 | 3 | 2–3 |
| B2 | 30–40 min | 5 | 3–4 | 3 |
| C1 | 35–45 min | 5–6 | 4 | 3–4 |
| C2 | 40–50 min | 6 | 4–5 | 4 |

A1 is deliberately absent. Every competency in all four libraries begins at A2, so there is no A1 curriculum to schedule. A true beginner tier would need its own libraries and is out of scope for version 1.

Notice what stays nearly constant:

* **4–6 speaking activities per day**  
* **30–45 minutes total**  
* The main difference is the **complexity** of the activities, not simply the quantity.

# **One more architectural idea**

I would avoid thinking of the curriculum as "Day 1, Day 2, Day 3..." in a rigid sequence.

Instead, think of it as a **playlist with prerequisites**.

Each practice session could be in one of four states:

Locked  
    ↓  
Available  
    ↓  
Completed  
    ↓  
Scheduled for Review

The 30-day plan is then simply the **initial schedule** through this playlist. If a learner struggles with a grammar concept, the adaptive review system can insert additional review sessions without disrupting the overall curriculum. If they master something quickly, they continue to the next available sessions. This gives you the predictability of a pre-generated plan while still allowing the system to personalize reinforcement where it matters most.

In other words:

* **Static:** curriculum, exercise templates, session schema.  
* **Generated occasionally:** a multi-week learning plan for a learner profile (level, goals, specialization).  
* **Adaptive daily:** spaced retrieval sessions and any extra reinforcement based on performance.

> **Superseded.** The middle layer — a stored multi-week learning plan — was dropped. A Day Plan reads mastery and retrievability that don't exist until the learner has practised, so a plan built in advance would have been either stale or fictional. What is built ahead is the *Stimulus Pool*: assets keyed by `(template, target_set, theme)`, topped up nightly, so the daily experience is still a database read. Everything above the Day Plan is computed on demand. See section 12 of `Session_Generation_Engine.md` and `docs/adr/0001-stimulus-pool-not-prebuilt-plans.md`.
